What Are Parallettes? Push-ups, Support Holds and the L-sit

Parallettes are a pair of short handles close to the floor. You grip them fully so your wrist stays straight, and use them for push-ups, support holds, leg raises and balance skills.

Pushing and controlBeginner to advancedParallettes

In short

  • On parallettes your wrist stays straight instead of bending back, which a wrist-force study concluded is likely safer [1].
  • Progressive push-ups really build strength: 3 days a week for 4 weeks raised strength like the bench press [2].
  • Start with push-ups and support holds, then a tuck hold, then the L-sit. WATHBA rule: do not move on before 3 clean 20-second holds at your current stage.
  • Train at least 2 sessions a week with 2–3 sets [3].
  • Parallettes do not treat wrist pain; if pain persists, stop and get it checked.

Target muscles

Primary muscles

  • In push-ups: chest, triceps and front shoulders [4] [5]
  • In supports and L-sits: triceps and shoulders hold the body up, abdominals and hip flexors lift the legs

Assisting and stabilising muscles

  • Forearms and grip: holding the handle throughout
  • Serratus anterior: steadies the shoulder blade in push-ups [6]

The push-up muscles come from the cited studies. The support and L-sit muscles are a WATHBA anatomical description, as we found no published study measuring these moves specifically.

How are parallettes different from parallel bars?

Parallettes are short and low, portable, and used for push-ups, supports and L-sits. Parallel bars are taller and used for full dips, because your body needs room to lower. Similar names, different jobs.

Why are parallettes easier on the wrist?

In a floor push-up the wrist bends back; on a handle it stays straight. A study measuring wrist forces in both positions concluded the straight wrist is likely safer, because ligaments may take more load when the wrist bends back [1].

The height also lets you lower deeper than the floor allows. A larger range builds more strength [3], but add depth gradually as your shoulders allow.

Steel with a wooden grip, or all wood?

WATHBA offers two options: steel bases with a beech grip, and all-beech parallettes. Steel is heavier and steadier for dynamic moves; wood is lighter to carry. Dimensions and details are on each product page.

The push-up stages follow the ACE chain [7]. The support and L-sit stages, the doses and the “Move on when” column are WATHBA working rules.

A starter week

At least two sessions a week, 2–3 sets per exercise [3]. In the progressive push-up study, participants trained 3 days a week [2].

Resting 60–90 seconds between sets is a WATHBA rule. Place the parallettes on level, non-slip ground and check they do not rock before every session.

Common mistakes and fixes

  • Shrugging in support. Push the handles down so your shoulders move away from your ears, and go back to feet-down support if you cannot.
  • Loading the edge of the handle. Grip the middle of the handle fully so your wrist stays straight [1].
  • Going for a full L-sit too soon. Start with tuck holds, then one leg at a time.
  • A slippery or uneven floor. Check the pair is steady before putting weight on it.

What do the sources say?

This is what each source behind this guide says, as stated in the source itself. Anything no source states, such as the move-on criteria and rest times, is a WATHBA working rule and is labelled as such.

  • J Hand Ther 2018 [1]: Compared push-ups on a hyperextended wrist with a neutral wrist and concluded that the neutral wrist is likely safer, because ligaments may be preferentially loaded in hyperextension.
  • J Strength Cond Res 2018 [2]: Progressive push-up variations, 3 days a week for 4 weeks, raised maximal bench-press strength much like bench-press training did. It was the first study to show that progressive calisthenics improves upper-body strength.
  • ACSM 2026 [3]: An overview of 137 systematic reviews with more than 30,000 participants. Strength improved more with heavier loads, a full range of motion, 2–3 sets and at least 2 sessions a week. Hypertrophy improved with 10 or more weekly sets per muscle and eccentric overload. Training to momentary failure did not consistently change outcomes.
  • ACE [4]: Targets arms, chest and shoulders. Brace the core, align the head with the spine, do not let the low back sag or the hips hike, and lower until the chest or chin touches the floor. Elbows close to the sides shift emphasis to the triceps and may reduce shoulder stress.
  • J Exerc Sci Fit 2017 [5]: Push-ups with an adjusted position, such as kneeling, twice a week for 8 weeks increased chest and triceps thickness and strength about as much as a bench press at a matched load.
  • Phys Sportsmed 2014 [6]: Elastic-resisted push-ups produced prime-mover activity similar to a heavy bench press while challenging the core more. Muscles measured included the chest, front deltoid, triceps, serratus anterior and abdominals.
  • ACE 2018 [7]: Example progression chains to be matched to each person. Pull-up: hollow hold, straight-arm then flexed-arm hang, eccentric-only, partial, band-assisted, bodyweight, then weighted pull-ups. Push-up: plank, push-up-position plank, incline push-up with the incline continually lowered, band-assisted, eccentric-only, bottom-up, bodyweight, then weighted push-ups. Squat: assisted squat, bodyweight squat, then loaded squats.

Frequently asked questions

Are parallettes only for advanced athletes?

No. Beginners start with knee push-ups and feet-down supports; advanced athletes train L-sits and balance skills. The movement changes, not the equipment.

Are parallettes push-ups better than floor push-ups?

The wrist stays straight, a position a study concluded is likely safer, and you can lower deeper. The muscles worked are the same.

Do parallettes fix wrist pain?

No. They may feel better because the wrist stays straight, but they do not treat an injury. If pain persists, stop and see a professional.

Can I do dips on parallettes?

Low parallettes do not give enough clearance for full dips. Use taller parallel bars for dips.

Steel or wood?

Steel with a wooden grip is heavier and steadier for dynamic moves; all-wood is lighter to carry. Choose by how and where you train.
