Calisthenics for Beginners: How to Start from Zero at Home

Calisthenics is strength training that uses your body weight instead of weights. You start with four movements, a push, a pull, a leg exercise and a trunk hold, and make each easier or harder by changing your body angle. You need to buy nothing in week one.

BeginnerBodyweight trainingAt home

In short

  • Calisthenics really builds strength: progressive push-up training 3 days a week for 4 weeks raised strength much like bench-press training [1].
  • Train all major muscle groups on at least 2 days a week [2] [3], and add 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity such as brisk walking each week [4].
  • Four movements are enough to start: a push-up, a pull, a squat and a plank. Each has an easier beginner version [5].
  • 2–3 sets per movement are enough, and you do not need to train to failure [3].
  • WATHBA rule: once you hit the top of the rep range cleanly in two sessions in a row, move to the harder version.

Target muscles

Primary muscles

  • Push: chest, triceps and front shoulders in the push-up [6]
  • Legs: thighs and glutes in the squat [7]
  • Pull: back and biceps in pull-ups and hangs [8]

Assisting and stabilising muscles

  • Trunk: abdominals and low back keep the body straight in every movement [6] [7]
  • Grip and forearms in hanging movements

These are the major muscle groups the NHS recommends training twice a week: legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms [2].

What is calisthenics?

Calisthenics is strength training where your body weight is the resistance. You change the difficulty with body angle and points of support: a wall push-up is lighter than a knee push-up, which is lighter than a full push-up [9].

Nor is it light training: push-ups at a matched load twice a week for 8 weeks grew the chest and triceps and their strength about as much as the bench press [10].

The four basic movements

Push: start with wall press-ups, then incline push-ups, lowering the surface over time [11] [5]. Details are in the push-up guide.

Legs: the bodyweight squat. Push your hips back and down and lower until your thighs are about parallel or your heels start to lift, knees tracking over the second toe [7]. If that is hard, sit down onto a chair and stand up slowly [11].

Pull: hang from a solid bar, then assisted pulls, as in the first pull-up guide [5]. Never improvise a hanging point on a door or furniture.

Trunk: the plank is the first step in the ACE push-up chain [5]. Start on your knees if needed, keeping your body in one line.

A starter week

Start with two sessions a week on non-consecutive days, each covering all four movements. That meets the advice to train every major muscle group twice a week [2] [3]. Add a third session after a month if you recover well.

Resting 60–120 seconds between sets is a WATHBA rule. Log each session: the movement, the reps and how hard it felt, and add one rep when you can.

Common mistakes and fixes

  • Starting with hard skills. Start each movement at its easy version and progress one step at a time; that is how progression chains are designed [5].
  • Training only chest and arms. Train legs, back and trunk too; the guidance covers every major muscle group [2].
  • Heels lifting or back rounding in the squat. Lower only until just before that happens [7], using a chair behind you as a target.
  • Training to exhaustion every day. Two or three sessions are enough, and training to failure does not reliably improve results [3].

What do the sources say?

This is what each source behind this guide says, as stated in the source itself. Anything no source states, such as the move-on criteria and rest times, is a WATHBA working rule and is labelled as such.

  • J Strength Cond Res 2018 [1]: Progressive push-up variations, 3 days a week for 4 weeks, raised maximal bench-press strength much like bench-press training did. It was the first study to show that progressive calisthenics improves upper-body strength.
  • NHS 2024 [2]: Strengthening activities for all major muscle groups (legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms) on at least 2 days a week, done to the point where you need a short rest before repeating.
  • ACSM 2026 [3]: An overview of 137 systematic reviews with more than 30,000 participants. Strength improved more with heavier loads, a full range of motion, 2–3 sets and at least 2 sessions a week. Hypertrophy improved with 10 or more weekly sets per muscle and eccentric overload. Training to momentary failure did not consistently change outcomes.
  • WHO 2020 [4]: All adults should do 150–300 minutes of moderate or 75–150 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity a week, and regular muscle-strengthening activity is recommended for every age group.
  • ACE 2018 [5]: Example progression chains to be matched to each person. Pull-up: hollow hold, straight-arm then flexed-arm hang, eccentric-only, partial, band-assisted, bodyweight, then weighted pull-ups. Push-up: plank, push-up-position plank, incline push-up with the incline continually lowered, band-assisted, eccentric-only, bottom-up, bodyweight, then weighted push-ups. Squat: assisted squat, bodyweight squat, then loaded squats.
  • ACE [6]: Targets arms, chest and shoulders. Brace the core, align the head with the spine, do not let the low back sag or the hips hike, and lower until the chest or chin touches the floor. Elbows close to the sides shift emphasis to the triceps and may reduce shoulder stress.
  • ACE [7]: A beginner exercise with no equipment. Feet slightly wider than hips; start by moving the hips back and down while the knees bend, back flat, and lower until the thighs are about parallel, or until the heels lift or the torso rounds. Knees track over the second toe; rise by pushing through the heels.
  • ACE [8]: Targets arms and back. Pull the shoulders back and down before pulling, avoid swinging, pull until the chin is level with the bar, then lower slowly until the arms are fully extended.
  • J Strength Cond Res 2011 [9]: Measured ground reaction force in 6 push-up variations. Knee push-ups and hands-elevated push-ups were lighter than the regular push-up; feet-elevated push-ups were heavier. No difference was found between men and women.
  • J Exerc Sci Fit 2017 [10]: Push-ups with an adjusted position, such as kneeling, twice a week for 8 weeks increased chest and triceps thickness and strength about as much as a bench press at a matched load.
  • NHS 2024 [11]: Seven beginner strength exercises, done at least twice a week while gradually adding repetitions, including: sit-to-stand 5 times, the slower the better; mini-squats 5 times; and wall press-ups, 3 sets of 5–10, at arm's length with hands at chest level and elbows by your sides.

Frequently asked questions

What are calisthenics exercises?

Bodyweight strength exercises such as push-ups, pull-ups, squats, planks and dips. You change their difficulty by changing body angle and support instead of adding weights.

Does calisthenics build muscle?

Yes. In one study, push-ups at a matched load twice a week for 8 weeks grew the chest and triceps about as much as the bench press; in another, progressive push-up training raised strength like the bench press did.

How often should I train?

At least 2 days a week for all major muscle groups, per the NHS and ACSM. For beginners we suggest two sessions on non-consecutive days, then three after a month.

Can I do calisthenics at home without equipment?

Yes for push-ups, squats and planks. Pulling needs a solid hanging point such as a properly fixed pull-up bar; never use doors or furniture.

Is calisthenics suitable for women?

Yes, with the same progression. WHO and NHS guidance covers all adults.

Do I need to do a pull-up on day one?

No. Start with hangs and assisted pulls, and follow the ladder in the first pull-up guide.
